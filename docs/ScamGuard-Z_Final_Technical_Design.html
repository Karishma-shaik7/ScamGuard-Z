Document Context & Team Baseline

This document details the complete design specification for ScamGuard-Z, created for the IBM Z Datathon 2026. The project is designed and executed by a team of six undergraduate students who are beginners in enterprise mainframe environments and advanced machine learning.

Accordingly, this report avoids exaggerated claims, unverified benchmarks, and premature assertions of completion. It establishes a rigorous, achievable engineering roadmap focused on a functional Minimum Working Prototype (MWP), while treating advanced extensions—such as machine learning benchmarking and IBM LinuxONE container deployment—as exploratory targets to be validated during the datathon.

Commitment to Technical Integrity All metrics, performance benchmarks, and deployment milestones presented herein are proposed design objectives. The prototype operates exclusively on synthetic data to prevent ethical compromises and ensure reproducible testing.

1. Executive Summary

In retail digital banking, Authorized Push Payment (APP) fraud and social engineering scams—such as impersonation of law enforcement ("digital arrest"), fake tax authorities, or urgent synthetic family emergencies—manipulate victims into executing and authenticating fund transfers themselves. Because the legitimate account owner enters their valid credentials, passwords, and two-factor authentication (2FA) codes while on their own trusted mobile or web device, conventional bank fraud engines designed primarily to stop unauthorized account takeovers often fail to identify that the user is acting under severe psychological coercion or deception.

ScamGuard-Z is an explainable transaction evaluation prototype designed to address this vulnerability. Designed specifically as an achievable, technically meaningful project for a six-member beginner student team, ScamGuard-Z analyzes simulated transactions across four observable risk factors: atypical transfer amounts, unfamiliar recipient history, rapid transfer velocity, and high account balance depletion ratios.

The system computes an explainable composite risk score ranging from 0 to 100, categorized into Low (0–39), Medium (40–69), and High (70–100) risk tiers. When compounding indicators trigger a High-Risk classification, the interface deploys an empathetic, practical safety warning modal encouraging the user to pause, question the caller's instructions, and verify recipient authenticity through independent channels. The project relies strictly on synthetic transaction data to protect consumer privacy, establishes a robust rule-based scoring baseline with an optional machine-learning extension, and targets the IBM LinuxONE Community Cloud (s390x) as an enterprise mainframe deployment platform to explore and validate during the datathon.

2. Problem Statement & Project Objectives

2.1 The Vulnerability of Authorized Coercion

Traditional banking fraud detection architectures are primarily engineered to prevent unauthorized access (e.g., credential stuffing, malware, or logins from unknown devices and unrecognized foreign IP addresses). However, social engineering attacks exploit the human element rather than technical system vulnerabilities. Scammers use high-pressure tactics, fabricated urgency, and fear to convince account holders to drain their savings accounts voluntarily. Because the legitimate account owner initiates and authorizes the transaction, conventional rule filters often treat the transfer as standard behavior.

2.2 Realistic Nuance: Suspicious Does Not Equal Scam

A foundational design principle of ScamGuard-Z is acknowledging that not every unusual transaction is a scam. Consumers frequently execute legitimate high-value or unfamiliar transfers—such as purchasing an automobile, making a home deposit, or sending money to a newly contracted service provider. Claiming that every anomaly is criminal would result in unacceptable user friction and false alarms. Therefore, ScamGuard-Z is not designed to unilaterally block accounts or prove psychological duress; rather, it serves as an informative, explainable advisory check that prompts users to pause and double-check when multiple risk indicators converge.

2.3 Core Project Objectives

  • Objective 1: Construct a clean web simulation interface allowing users and evaluators to input mock payment details.
  • Objective 2: Develop an explainable rule-based risk-scoring engine evaluating four observable transaction factors.
  • Objective 3: Categorize risk into Low (0–39), Medium (40–69), and High (70–100) tiers accompanied by clear explanations.
  • Objective 4: Implement an empathetic safety intervention modal providing practical verification steps.
  • Objective 5: Explore an optional Scikit-Learn machine-learning extension to benchmark against the rule-based engine if time permits.
  • Objective 6: Explore packaging the backend into a container and deploying it on the IBM LinuxONE Community Cloud.

3. Proposed Solution & Core Prototype Features

To ensure the project remains achievable and verifiable during a 24-hour datathon, ScamGuard-Z clearly separates its Minimum Working Prototype (MWP) from optional exploration tracks:

1. Simulation & Input Interface (Core MWP)

A simple web form enabling users to test simulated transactions by inputting payment parameters: transfer amount, account balance prior to payment, transfer frequency in the last 24 hours, and whether the recipient is newly added or previously verified.

2. Explainable Risk-Scoring Engine (Core MWP)

A deterministic baseline engine that calculates a composite Risk Score (0–100) based on transparent, inspectable mathematical weights across four key detection factors, ensuring total auditability and zero black-box obscurity.

3. Tiered Risk Assessment (Core MWP)

Classifies each simulated transfer into actionable categories:
• Low Risk (0–39): Routine characteristics; standard proceed confirmation.
• Medium Risk (40–69): Isolated unusual attribute; gentle advisory note.
• High Risk (70–100): Multiple compounding risk factors; prominent safety warning.

4. Practical Safety Warning Modal (Core MWP)

Instead of abrupt account locks, High-Risk transfers trigger a clear educational dialog:
• "Are you on the phone with someone telling you to send this urgently?"
• "Did the caller claim to be police, tax staff, or bank fraud security?"
• Practical verification checklist and one-click "Cancel & Review" option.

5. Optional Exploration Tracks (Time & Data Permitting) As secondary objectives, the team will explore: (a) training an optional binary classifier (Scikit-Learn Logistic Regression or Random Forest) on synthetic data to compare against rule weights, and (b) deploying the FastAPI backend to an enterprise Linux on Z virtual machine via the IBM LinuxONE Community Cloud.

3.1 Detailed Evaluation of Detection Factors

In social engineering scams, coercive actors typically instruct victims to transfer as much money as possible, quickly, and to newly controlled intermediary or "mule" accounts. ScamGuard-Z identifies these behavioral indicators through four observable signals:

Detection Factor Mathematical Representation Operational Rationale Simulated Risk Indicator
1. Atypical Transfer Amount Factor score $S_{\text{amt}} \in [0, 1]$ scaled by ratio of transfer amount to 30-day historical average. Coercive scammers push victims to make unusually large lump-sum payments under false legal or financial threats. Transfer value exceeds 3× to 5× the account's historical median payment.
2. Unfamiliar Recipient Status Factor score $S_{\text{recip}} \in [0, 1]$ based on payee age and prior transaction history. Victims are directed to send funds to newly created third-party accounts or unfamiliar payee accounts. Beneficiary added within past 24 hours with zero previous payment records.
3. Transfer Velocity (Frequency) Factor score $S_{\text{vel}} \in [0, 1]$ proportional to outbound transfers within past 24 hours. Scammers often conduct rapid sequential transfers ("test transfers" followed by full liquidation). Multiple outbound transfers executed within minutes or hours of each other.
4. Account Depletion Ratio Factor score $S_{\text{dep}} \in [0, 1]$ calculated as $\text{Amount} / \text{Available Balance}$. Extortionists typically attempt to drain nearly all available liquid capital before the victim realizes the deception. Single transaction attempts to liquidate 75% to 95% of available liquid balance.

Factor Normalization and Threshold Mapping

To ensure mathematical consistency across disparate units (currency amounts, time intervals, and account percentages), each factor is mapped into a normalized score between 0.0 and 1.0:

  • Amount Factor ($S_{\text{amt}}$): If $\text{Amount} \le \text{Average}$, $S_{\text{amt}} = 0.0$. If $\text{Amount} \ge 4 \times \text{Average}$, $S_{\text{amt}} = 1.0$. Intermediate values scale linearly between 0.0 and 1.0.
  • Recipient Factor ($S_{\text{recip}}$): Payee age < 24 hours with zero history yields $S_{\text{recip}} = 1.0$. Payee age between 24–72 hours yields $0.5$. Established payees with prior verified transfers yield $0.0$.
  • Velocity Factor ($S_{\text{vel}}$): 1 transfer today yields $0.0$. 2 transfers yield $0.4$. 3 transfers yield $0.7$. 4 or more outbound transfers within 24 hours yield $S_{\text{vel}} = 1.0$.
  • Depletion Factor ($S_{\text{dep}}$): Ratio < 25% yields $0.0$. Ratio between 25% and 80% scales linearly from $0.1$ to $0.8$. Ratio $\ge 80\%$ yields $S_{\text{dep}} = 1.0$.
Design Principle: Transparent Heuristics By normalizing every factor strictly to the $[0, 1]$ domain, the scoring engine guarantees that no single factor can exceed its allotted weight, preventing unpredictable edge-case behavior during simulation.

4. System Architecture & Workflow

The architecture of ScamGuard-Z separates the frontend presentation, backend scoring services, and deployment target to ensure clean modularity and straightforward testing for beginner developers.

4.1 System Architecture Diagram

Figure 1: High-level ScamGuard-Z architecture from simulated input to risk output.

Component Roles

  • Frontend Web Client: Captures user-submitted transaction parameters and displays the corresponding risk badge and warning modal.
  • Feature Extractor: Translates raw transaction inputs into normalized ratios ($S_{\text{amt}}, S_{\text{recip}}, S_{\text{vel}}, S_{\text{dep}}$).
  • Risk Scoring Engine: Executes the weighted mathematical formula, generating a score between 0 and 100.
  • Reasoning Generator: Compiles contextual strings explaining exactly why the score was assigned.

4.2 Risk Assessment Sequential Workflow

The following diagram illustrates the linear decision pipeline executed on every transaction submission, from feature extraction to tier classification and safety intervention:

Figure 2: Sequential transaction evaluation and decision workflow.

Step-by-Step Execution Protocol

  1. Payload Ingestion: The web client posts JSON payload to `/evaluate-transaction` containing simulated transfer fields.
  2. Statistical Normalization: Feature extractor compares input fields against synthetic baseline history, generating factor scores $S_i \in [0, 1]$.
  3. Deterministic Weighting: Scoring function calculates $\text{Risk Score} = 30 S_{\text{amt}} + 25 S_{\text{recip}} + 25 S_{\text{vel}} + 20 S_{\text{dep}}$.
  4. Tier Assignment: Evaluates score against static boundaries: Low (<40), Medium (40–69), High (≥70).
  5. Client Intervention Rendering: The web interface displays the corresponding badge and, if High Risk, overlays the safety warning dialog.
Auditability & Transparency Because every step in the pipeline produces inspectable JSON outputs, judges and evaluators can verify the mathematical calculations in real time via the FastAPI documentation page (`/docs`).

6. Data Strategy & Synthetic Dataset Generation

6.1 Synthetic Transaction Data as the Primary Source

Connecting to real bank APIs or collecting actual customer financial records poses severe privacy, legal, and ethical risks. Furthermore, genuine coercion and scam transactions are rarely labeled in public datasets due to bank confidentiality. Therefore, ScamGuard-Z uses 100% synthetically generated transaction data as its primary foundation.

Using a Python script, our team will generate synthetic datasets representing simulated account histories and payment requests:

  • Normal Simulated Patterns: Routine transactions reflecting historical behaviors—modest transfer amounts (e.g., $20 to $150), recurring familiar recipients (e.g., utility providers, saved contacts), low transaction frequency (1–2 per day), and small account depletion ratios (<15%).
  • Suspicious Simulated Scenarios: Parameterized to mimic common social engineering patterns—sudden high-value transfers ($2,000+), newly registered recipient accounts (<24 hours old), multiple transfers in rapid succession, and transactions that liquidate >80% of available account funds.

6.2 Critical Distinction: Simulated Risk vs. Verified Fraud

Academic & Technical Rigor: Simulated Labels vs. Convicted Fraud Labels in our synthetic dataset denote simulated risk profiles based on controlled behavioral parameters, not ground-truth criminal convictions. This distinction prevents circular reasoning and ensures evaluators understand the boundary between heuristic anomaly testing and true real-world fraud forensics.

6.3 Exploratory Public Datasets (Subject to Availability & Licensing)

The team will explore publicly accessible financial benchmarks, such as the Kaggle IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection dataset, solely as a reference point for feature distributions and tabular structures. Its use will remain strictly subject to dataset availability, download bandwidth constraints during the hackathon, and platform licensing terms. Synthetic data will remain our primary testing source regardless of external dataset accessibility.

7. AI & Risk Scoring Methodology

7.1 Rule-Based Baseline Engine (Primary Methodology)

For a team of beginners, starting with a transparent, deterministic rule-based engine ensures reliability, auditability, and immediate explainability. The scoring engine evaluates four factors, each assigned a normalized sub-score between 0.0 and 1.0, multiplied by fixed integer weights that sum to 100:

Risk Score Formulation:
$$\text{Risk Score} = 30 \times S_{\text{amt}} + 25 \times S_{\text{recip}} + 25 \times S_{\text{vel}} + 20 \times S_{\text{dep}}$$
• Factor Scores: Each $S_i \in [0.0, 1.0]$ represents the severity of that specific risk indicator.
• Weight Distribution: $30 + 25 + 25 + 20 = 100$ points total.
• Final Score Range: Bounded strictly between $0$ and $100$.
• Proposed Risk Tiers: Low (0–39), Medium (40–69), and High (70–100).

7.2 Mathematically Consistent Example Calculation

Consider a simulated social engineering scenario where an elderly user is coerced into wiring savings to an unfamiliar "verification" account:

  • Transfer Amount: $4,500 (4.5× the user's historical median of $1,000) $\implies S_{\text{amt}} = 1.0 \implies 30 \times 1.0 = 30.0$
  • Recipient Status: Added 2 hours ago with zero prior transfer history $\implies S_{\text{recip}} = 1.0 \implies 25 \times 1.0 = 25.0$
  • Transfer Velocity: First transfer executed today $\implies S_{\text{vel}} = 0.0 \implies 25 \times 0.0 = 0.0$
  • Balance Depletion: Current balance is $5,000; transfer liquidates 90% of funds $\implies S_{\text{dep}} = 1.0 \implies 20 \times 1.0 = 20.0$

Calculated Total Score: $30 + 25 + 0 + 20 = \mathbf{75}$ (Classified as High Risk).

7.3 Explainability Mechanism

Whenever an individual factor score exceeds its activation threshold, the engine attaches a human-readable explanation string to the JSON response. In the example above, the client receives:

  • "Transfer amount ($4,500) is 4.5× higher than your 30-day average."
  • "Recipient account was added 2 hours ago with zero prior transfer history."
  • "This transaction transfers 90% of your total available account balance."

7.4 Optional Machine-Learning Extension (Time Permitting)

If the baseline is operational ahead of schedule, the team plans to train a basic binary classifier (e.g., Logistic Regression or Random Forest from Scikit-Learn) on synthetic data. This extension is proposed strictly as an exploratory exercise to compare algorithmic feature importance against our rule weights. No model has been trained or deployed prior to the datathon.

9. Privacy, Security & Ethical Considerations

Because financial applications handle sensitive human situations, ScamGuard-Z adheres strictly to ethical development practices appropriate for a student hackathon:

  • Synthetic Data Exclusivity: The prototype never connects to live banking rails, real bank accounts, or real payment credentials. All names, balances, and account numbers are fictional.
  • Data Minimization: The engine processes only essential transaction parameters (amount, timestamp delta, recipient status, balance percentage) without capturing or storing personal identifying information (PII).
  • Non-Coercive User Agency: The system does not freeze accounts permanently. It introduces an intentional cognitive speed-bump, respecting customer autonomy while providing critical safety advice.
  • Absence of Unsupported Claims: The project does not claim compliance with PCI-DSS or GDPR certification, but adopts privacy-by-design principles suited for a prototype.

12. Limitations & Future Scope

12.1 Prototype Limitations

  • Cannot Prove True Coercion: A software algorithm operating solely on transaction metadata cannot determine a user's psychological state or verify what is happening off-device on a telephone call.
  • Potential for False Positives: Legitimate urgent transactions (e.g., emergency medical bill payment) share attributes with coercion fraud (high amounts, urgent timing), leading to advisory warnings on legitimate transfers.
  • Does Not Replace Bank Systems: ScamGuard-Z is an experimental educational concept designed to augment human awareness, not replace professional fraud departments, human investigators, or established anti-money laundering (AML) controls.

12.2 Future Scope (Post-Datathon Opportunities)

  • Multi-Party Verification: Exploring opt-in "trusted contact" verification where high-risk transactions notify a pre-selected family member for advisory confirmation.
  • Enterprise Mainframe Integration: Deepening technical validation on IBM Z systems to examine real-time transactional throughput and hardware-level cryptographic services.
  • Ethical Behavioral Biometrics: Investigating whether ethical, client-side input hesitation patterns (e.g., typing pauses during payee entry) can add non-intrusive safety signals.
